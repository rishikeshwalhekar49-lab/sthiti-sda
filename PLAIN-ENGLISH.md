# Sthiti, explained without jargon

For anyone — family, a non-finance examiner, a friend who has never bought a share. No formulas, no
assumed knowledge. Every technical word is explained the first time it appears.

---

## The whole thing in one paragraph

People who want to learn the stock market in India have two kinds of help available, and neither
works on its own. There are **courses**, which explain ideas using made-up examples. And there are
**screeners** — websites that show you walls of live numbers about real companies, with no
explanation of what any of it means. A beginner finishes the course and still cannot read the
screen. Sthiti puts the two together: every lesson ends on a real number from today's market, and
every number on the screen can be tapped to explain itself. Then it adds a third thing that nobody
else sells: a system that watches the *mood* of the whole market and tells you when the usual rules
have stopped working.

---

## Part 1 — The problem, properly

Imagine learning to drive from a textbook. You learn what a clutch does, what a roundabout is, what
the signs mean. Then someone puts you on a real road at rush hour. The textbook was true, but it
did not prepare you for the road.

Stock-market education in India is like that. Courses teach five subjects: company accounts, price
charts, options, risk, and the psychology of not panicking. All useful. But the examples are tidy
and invented, and the real screen is messy and live.

There is a second problem, and it is bigger. **Courses teach rules as though the market always
behaves the same way.** It doesn't. There are long stretches where different parts of the market go
their own way — technology does one thing, banks another, cement another. And there are stretches
where everything moves together, usually when people are frightened. The same rule that works in the
first kind of market can fail badly in the second. No course mentions which kind of market you are
in today, because until now nothing told them.

## Part 2 — What we built, in three layers

### Layer one: a path, not a pile

Twelve short lessons in three stages. Stage one assumes you have never bought a share and explains
what you actually own, how to read a price, what makes a company worth something, and what the whole
market did today. Stage two is method: trends, momentum, and — most importantly — how to decide the
size of a bet before you make it. Stage three is the advanced material: options, and the market-mood
engine itself.

Each lesson takes a few minutes, ends with two questions so you can check you understood, and then
hands you the one tool on the site where you use it. You see your progress; you always know what
comes next.

### Layer two: tools that read today's real market

Ten of them. The screener shows real companies with real numbers, and **every number is a button**:
tap it and it explains that number for that company, today. Not "P/E means price divided by
earnings" — rather "this company costs 15 rupees for every rupee it earns in a year, while its
industry as a whole costs 17.8, so it is priced below its peers."

There is a calculator that works out how many shares to buy from how much you can afford to lose.
There is a simulator showing exactly what an options bet pays before you place it. There is a
scanner that checks all 500 big Indian companies every day for the patterns the course taught you.

This is the original part, and the next section explains it with no mathematics at all.

## Part 3 — The engine, explained with a crowd

### What "moving together" means

Take two companies. On days when one rises, does the other usually rise too? If yes, they move
together. If one zigs while the other zags, they move apart. That is the only measurement the engine
makes, and it makes it for every pair.

### Turn it into a map

Now imagine a map where **distance means disagreement**. Two companies that always move together
stand on the same spot. Two that go their own ways stand far apart. Do this for the seventeen big
sectors of the Indian market — banks, technology, cars, cement, pharma and so on — and you get a map
of the market.

On a normal day the map is spread out: sectors stand in their own corners. In a panic, everyone runs
to the same spot, because in a crash *everything* falls together. The map collapses.

### Now squint

Here is the clever part, and the part that is genuinely new in finance.

Stand back from the map and squint. Points that are close together blur into a single blob. Squint
harder and more blobs merge, until finally everything is one. **The interesting question is not how
many blobs there are — it is how long each blob survives as you keep squinting.**

A grouping that disappears the moment you squint was never real; it was an accident of where you
drew the line. A grouping that survives a lot of squinting is genuine structure.

The engine squints gradually and writes down, for every group, when it appeared and when it merged.
That list is the **barcode** you see on the screen: one bar per group, and the length of the bar is
how long that group survived. Long bars mean a market with real, separate parts. Short bars mean a
market that is really just one thing wearing different hats.

### Rings

Sometimes something stranger shows up. Five sectors can form a **ring**: each one close to its
neighbours, but nobody standing in the middle. Think of five towns around a lake, each an hour from
the next, with no road across the water. The engine spots these rings too, and records when each one
opened and when it finally filled in.

Rings matter because they are the shape of rotation — money moving around the market from one sector
to the next in a cycle, rather than everything moving at once.

### The verdict

From all of that the engine produces one number: on average, how closely are the sectors moving
together right now? Then it compares that number with the last two years:

- **Bottom third → Calm.** Sectors are going their own way. Picking individual companies pays off,
  and the ordinary chart rules behave the way the course describes.
- **Middle third → Transitional.** Things are drifting. Be more careful; check signals twice.
- **Top third → Stress.** Everything is moving as one. This is the important one, and the reason is
  worth spelling out.

### Why "everything moving together" is the warning

The standard safety advice is: don't put all your money in one company, spread it across many. That
advice only works **because different companies move differently**. If one falls, another holds.

When the market enters a stress state, that stops being true. Twenty different companies start
behaving like one company. Your money looks spread out and isn't — and this happens precisely when
markets are falling, which is exactly when you thought you were protected.

That is the single most useful thing the engine says, and it is why the site changes what it shows
you depending on the state. In a calm market it points you at company analysis. In a stressed one it
points you at protecting what you have.

## Part 4 — The ten tools, one line each

1. **Screener and health scorecard** — real companies, real numbers, and every number explains
   itself for that company. The scorecard then runs five simple checks (is it expensive? is it
   profitable? is it trending up?) and shows which pass.
2. **Chart with momentum marks** — a year of real prices, with every day the stock became
   "overbought" or "oversold" shaded, so you see how often that signal was right.
3. **Market mood meter** — today's market in one reading, from three real numbers: how many shares
   rose versus fell, whether big institutions bought or sold, and how nervous the options market is.
   It then explains that reading in a sentence.
4. **Position calculator** — the most valuable tool for a beginner. You say how much money you have
   and how much of it you are willing to lose on one trade; it tells you how many shares to buy. If
   the share is a jumpy one, it automatically tells you to buy fewer.
5. **Option simulator** — options are contracts that make most beginners lose money. This draws the
   exact picture of what you would make or lose, using today's real option prices.
6. **Scanner** — checks all 500 large Indian companies every day for the specific patterns the
   course taught, and lists the ones that triggered, with how unusual their trading volume was.
7. **Topo-Screen** — ranks companies by how independently they move from the rest of the market.
   Useful when you want something that will not fall with everything else.
8. **Regime backtester** — takes a classic strategy, applies it to two years of real history across
   500 companies, and shows how it performed separately in calm markets and in stressed ones.
9. **Portfolio check** — paste in what you own; it tells you whether your holdings are genuinely
   spread out or only look that way. The nine-stock example on the site turns out to behave like
   about four and a half independent holdings, and it names the pair responsible — two technology
   companies that have been moving almost as one.
10. **Practice trading** — buy and sell at real live prices with imaginary money, and then it reads
    your own record back to you: do you hold your losers too long? Do you bet bigger after a loss?
    Almost everyone does both, and most people never notice.

## Part 5 — The seven original ideas, in plain words

These are the seven things we think are new enough to patent. Each one runs on the site.

1. **Updating instead of recalculating.** Working out the market's shape from scratch every time new
   prices arrive is slow. This updates only what changed — like keeping a running total instead of
   re-adding a long column of numbers. *Honest finding:* we measured it, and the easy half gets about
   four times faster while the hard half needs more work than we have done. We say so on the page.
2. **Naming who caused it.** Existing research announces that the market's shape changed. It never
   says which sectors changed it. Ours names them and ranks them — the difference between a doctor
   saying "you're unwell" and saying "it's your left lung".
3. **Watching two things at once.** Measuring only how closely things move can't tell a panic apart
   from a quiet squeeze in one corner of the market. This watches co-movement *and* nervousness
   together, the way you'd judge a house on price *and* distance from work rather than either alone.
4. **The system tuning itself.** Any such measure needs a setting: how many days to look back. Too
   short and it panics at noise; too long and it notices a crash three weeks late. The system tries
   seven settings on real history and picks the steadiest one that still reacts.
5. **Checking the alarm before ringing it.** Before announcing that something changed, it scrambles
   the data — twice, in two different ways — and checks how often pure chance produces what it just
   saw. Only if chance almost never does will it raise the alert. This is what stops a system from
   crying wolf.
6. **Following a shock between asset classes.** Trouble rarely stays in shares. This watches shares,
   government bonds, corporate bonds, property trusts and the market's fear gauge in a single
   picture, and tracks the path a shock takes between them.
7. **Reading the options market's shape.** Option prices across different price levels and dates
   form a landscape with valleys and ridges. The depth and number of valleys says whether traders
   share one view of the future or several competing ones — and the options market often moves
   before share prices do.

## Part 6 — The surprise we did not hide

We tested one of the oldest rules in chart reading — the "golden cross", a signal that is supposed
to mean a stock is turning upward — across 500 companies and two years, splitting every trade by
what state the market was in that day.

In calm markets it won 29% of the time and lost money. In stressed markets it won 52% and made
money.

That is the **opposite** of what we assumed when we built it. We expected stressed markets to be the
ones you filter out. We left the result on the screen exactly as it came, with the caveats attached:
314 trades is a small sample, and we did not deduct brokerage or taxes. It is a finding, not advice.

The deeper point survives either way: **the state of the market changes the answer**, and nothing
else available to an Indian retail investor tells them what state they are in.

## Part 7 — Where the numbers come from, and what we cannot do

**The data is real and it is public.** India's National Stock Exchange publishes a file every
evening with the day's prices for every listed company, and another with every index. It also
publishes live index levels, how many shares rose and fell, and what foreign and domestic
institutions bought. The Bombay Stock Exchange publishes live share prices and company figures.
The site reads all of that directly. Nothing on it is invented or copied from a textbook.

**The honest limits**, all stated on the site itself:

- Two figures a course would want — company debt levels and profit growth — are not in any free
  source. We leave them blank and say why, rather than guessing.
- Share prices update at the end of each day, plus the live price while the market is open. We do
  not have second-by-second data; that costs money.
- This reads public web endpoints. A real product would need a paid, licensed data feed — both
  exchanges restrict automated access.
- India's market regulator, SEBI, has rules about teaching with recent price data. Before this
  becomes a business, a securities lawyer needs to look at it. We wrote that into the plan rather
  than discovering it later.
- **Nothing on the site tells anyone to buy or sell anything.** Every tool explains a number and
  points at the lesson behind it. That is a deliberate line, and it is also the safer side of the
  regulatory question.

## Glossary

| Word | What it means here |
|---|---|
| Share | A slice of ownership in a company |
| NSE / BSE | India's two stock exchanges |
| Index (NIFTY 50) | A single number summarising a group of companies |
| P/E | How many rupees you pay for each rupee the company earns in a year |
| Volatility | How much something jumps around |
| India VIX | How much movement traders expect over the next month |
| Breadth | How many shares rose versus how many fell |
| FII / DII | Foreign and Indian institutional investors — the big buyers |
| Option | A contract giving the right, not the obligation, to buy or sell at a set price |
| Correlation | Whether two things tend to move together |
| Regime | The market's current behaviour pattern — its weather |
| Topology | The study of shape: what stays true about something when you squint at it |
| Persistence | How long a grouping survives as you squint harder — the test of whether it is real |
| Barcode | The written record of every grouping: when it appeared, when it merged |
| Backtest | Running a rule against past data to see how it would have done |
